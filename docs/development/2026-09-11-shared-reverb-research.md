# Shared stereo musical reverb research

## Scope and assumptions

This note compares four algorithm families for one session-owned stereo reverb in moondsp. A part contributes a send to that instance; this is not a general effects graph, arbitrary routing, or multiple simultaneous spaces. The audio host is assumed to run at 48 kHz in browser-sized blocks of up to 128 frames. The reverb must retain its tail after note-off, section changes, and live parameter edits, but an explicit `Stop` must silence immediately and clear the tail.

`[Source]` marks a fact in the cited paper, first-party implementation, or repository source. `[Inference]` marks an engineering conclusion for this product.

## Primary sources and what they establish

### Schroeder (1962)

Manfred R. Schroeder, “Natural Sounding Artificial Reverberation,” *Journal of the Audio Engineering Society* 10(3), pp. 219–223, July 1962, Bell Telephone Laboratories ([AES record](https://aes2.org/publications/elibrary-page/?id=849); [scan](https://hajim.rochester.edu/ece/sites/zduan/teaching/ece472/reading/Schroeder_1962.pdf)). `[Source]` The design is parallel feedback comb filters followed by cascaded delay allpasses. The combs provide decay; the allpasses increase echo density without being the decay control. `[Source]` For a delay of `M` samples at sample rate `Fs`, the paper's relationship is `T60 = 3(M/Fs) / log10(1/|g|)`, so a desired decay time gives `|g| = 10^(-3M/(Fs T60))`.

`[Inference]` This is the smallest useful reference design, but four combs and two allpasses have the least density and the most obvious periodic/metallic coloration. Stereo requires duplicated or coupled networks and deliberately different delays; the original paper's ambiophonic discussion is not a ready-made modern stereo image strategy.

### Moorer (1979) and first-party STK implementations

James A. Moorer, “About This Reverberation Business,” *Computer Music Journal* 3(2), pp. 13–28, June 1979 ([JSTOR record](https://www.jstor.org/stable/3680280); [author's article page](https://www.jamminpower.org/Technical%20Articles.html); [author-hosted PDF](https://www.jamminpower.org/PDF/reverberation%20business.pdf)). `[Source]` Moorer's practical design adds a first-order low-pass filter in each feedback-comb loop, then combines the late output with an allpass stage; the paper also treats early reflections separately from the late field. The low-pass makes high frequencies decay faster, rather than using one broadband decay for the whole tail.

CCRMA's first-party [STK `NRev` implementation](https://ccrma.stanford.edu/software/stk/classstk_1_1NRev.html) is a useful implementation record: `[Source]` it exposes `T60`, takes mono input, and produces stereo from six parallel combs, three allpasses, a low-pass stage, another allpass, and two final parallel allpasses. `[Inference]` Moorer's separation of decay and damping is the cleanest conceptual fit for the requested `decay` and high-frequency-damping controls, but early-reflection taps are outside the initial product scope and make the smallest implementation larger.

### Freeverb (Jezar, 2000)

The archival mirror of Jezar at Dreampoint's public-domain source is [sinshu/freeverb](https://github.com/sinshu/freeverb), with the exact [comb](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sinshu/freeverb/master/Components/comb.hpp), [allpass](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sinshu/freeverb/master/Components/allpass.hpp), [model](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sinshu/freeverb/master/Components/revmodel.cpp), and [tuning](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sinshu/freeverb/master/Components/tuning.h) files. `[Source]` The June 2000 README calls it a Schroeder/Moorer model with eight low-pass feedback combs per channel followed by four allpasses per channel. Right-channel delays are offset by 23 samples; the delay table is listening-tuned for 44.1 kHz. Its source uses allpass feedback `0.5`, input gain `0.015`, a room-size value as comb feedback, and a one-pole comb damping state. It deliberately has no predelay or EQ.

Two independent first-party API records corroborate the design: CCRMA's [STK `FreeVerb`](https://ccrma.stanford.edu/software/stk/classstk_1_1FreeVerb.html) documents eight low-pass-feedback combs plus four allpasses and the 44.1 kHz tuning; JUCE's [official `Reverb` documentation](https://docs.juce.com/master/classjuce_1_1Reverb.html) describes its simple stereo reverb as based on FreeVerb technique and tunings. `[Source]` The original Freeverb code also explicitly calls `undenormalise` on comb/allpass state, demonstrating that very small IIR values need a finite/denormal policy.

`[Inference]` Freeverb is the best established small stereo compromise: enough parallel paths for ordinary songs, enough diffusion for long ambient pads, and stereo decorrelation that is explicit rather than accidental. Its fixed tunings are not a physical room model, but that is an advantage for this one-session scope.

### Feedback delay networks (FDNs)

John Stautner and Miller Puckette, “Designing Multi-Channel Reverberators,” *Computer Music Journal* 6(1), pp. 52–65, Spring 1982 ([Puckette's publication list](https://msp.ucsd.edu/publications.html); [stable scan](https://www.ee.columbia.edu/~dpwe/papers/StautP82-reverb.pdf)), and Jean-Marc Jot and Antoine Chaigne, “Digital Delay Networks for Designing Artificial Reverberators,” AES Convention 90, Preprint 3030, February 1991 ([AES record](https://aes2.org/publications/elibrary-page/?id=5663)). `[Source]` These works formulate multiple delay lines with a feedback/mixing matrix. An orthogonal/unitary matrix redistributes energy; a separate contractive gain or loss filter controls decay, allowing structure and decay design to be separated. `[Source]` Jot and Chaigne specifically describe extending the method to recursive delay networks with unitary feedback and frequency-dependent loss.

Miller Puckette's first-party [artificial-reverberation chapter](https://msp.ucsd.edu/techniques/v0.07/book-html/node114.html) and official Pd [ `rev2~.pd` patch](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pure-data/pure-data/master/extra/rev2~.pd) show a compact four-delay realization using rotations and normalized feedback. The chapter states that, in that realization, feedback gain `1/2` is the perpetual/lossless limit; it also recommends roughly exponentially growing delay times and low-pass filters for faster high-frequency decay. `[Inference]` A small FDN can sound more diffuse and decorrelated than comb banks, but matrix choice, delay selection, loss filters, and output taps are a larger tuning and stability surface. Dense FDNs also cost `O(N^2)` mixing per sample unless the matrix is structured.

## Practical coefficient and integration rules

1. **Decay.** For every fixed delay `M_i`, calculate `g_i = 10^(-3M_i/(Fs T60_low))`. This is the Schroeder relationship and gives the same low-frequency/DC `T60` across unequal delays. `[Inference]` For a 1,440-sample (30 ms) line at 48 kHz and `T60 = 2 s`, `g = 0.902`; the repository's `DelayLine` feedback limit of `0.99` bounds the longest-tail setting to a finite value rather than allowing a marginal/infinite loop.
2. **Damping.** Use a normalized one-pole state `H(z) = (1-a)/(1-a z^-1)` in each comb loop, with `0 <= a < 1`; `a = 0` is bright/no added damping and larger `a` damps highs more. `[Inference]` Because `H(1)=1`, the decay calculation above still controls low-frequency `T60`; at other frequencies the loop magnitude is `|g_i H(e^{jω})|`, so highs decay sooner. A small dedicated filtered-comb state is more faithful and cheaper than forcing the existing `Biquad` into this role.
3. **48 kHz and stereo.** Scale the Freeverb 44.1 kHz delay table by `Fs/44100` and round once during session construction; preserve the right-side offset after scaling. Allocate all delay/filter state before the audio callback. Do not change delay lengths in a live edit; change only bounded coefficients and ramp them over blocks if needed to avoid a zipper transient. These are engineering inferences from the fixed-delay source and the repository's preallocated `DelayLine`.
4. **Finite output and denormals.** Sanitize send inputs and every output/state update for finite values. If a value is non-finite, clear the affected state and emit zero for the rest of the block. Clamp tiny magnitudes to zero (the original Freeverb source's `undenormalise` is the relevant precedent). The existing `Biquad` invalidates non-finite state, but `DelayLine::tick` itself does not make the whole reverb finite-safe.

## Lifetime and recommendation

`[Inference]` Session ownership plus continuous zero-input processing is the correct lifetime model. Note-off and section changes set sends to zero; they do not reset the reverb, so the IIR tail remains audible. A 128-frame block must still advance all delay/filter state with zero input until the tail is below the chosen silence threshold. A live edit updates bounded decay/damping coefficients while retaining state. Explicit `Stop` is the sole hard lifetime boundary: clear every delay/filter state and return an all-zero block immediately, rather than allowing the old tail to leak.

Implement a single shared **Freeverb-style stereo network**: eight parallel filtered combs and four allpasses per side, fixed delays scaled from the published 44.1 kHz table, a 23-sample right-side decorrelation offset, and a dedicated one-pole damping state per comb. Expose per-part send level(s), `T60`, and damping; derive each comb feedback from `T60` rather than reusing Freeverb's non-physical room-size mapping. Keep all coefficients finite and strictly contractive. This is small enough for browser blocks, materially denser and more stereo-stable than Schroeder, and adjustable from short ordinary-song tails through long ambient tails.

Rejected alternatives: bare Schroeder is too colored; a full Moorer implementation adds early-reflection scope and a filter/read-write seam without a decisive benefit over the tuned Freeverb network; a general FDN is deferred because its extra matrix/tank design surface is not justified before listening evidence. If ambient listening still exposes periodic ringing, the next experiment should be a small unitary FDN—not a generic routing abstraction.
